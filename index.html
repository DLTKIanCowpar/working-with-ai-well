Prompt engineering is a skill. Like any skill, it gets better with practice.

If it feels like other people are getting more out of AI than you are, it's probably not you. Most of us were never actually shown what makes a prompt work, we just started typing and hoped for the best. This page is here to help with that: clearer requests, fewer rounds of back-and-forth, better answers the first time, whether you're in Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, or whatever comes next.

This page is a curated set of practices for getting more out of whatever you're working in. Not a rulebook, not specific to one tool. We pulled from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, creators on LinkedIn and YouTube, and the open prompting community, then organized it by how much effort each habit takes and how much it actually improves your results.

Quick hits

These hold true whether you're in Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, or whatever comes next. You don't need to adopt all eight at once, even picking up one or two makes a real difference. Tagged below by the kind of difference each one makes.

Speed

Start fresh for a new task

Old context that doesn't relate to your current ask just adds noise for the model to wade through. A new chat is often faster and gives you a cleaner answer than dragging an old one along.

Speed

Use a reusable workspace for recurring work

Most tools have some version of this: Projects, custom GPTs, Gems. Set it up once and every future ask starts from a cleaner baseline instead of a blank paste.

Speed

Match the model to the task

Not every task needs the most capable model available. Lighter models are often faster too, save the heavy-duty option for the work that actually needs the extra horsepower.

Flow

Batch related questions

If you have three related asks, send them in one message instead of three. Planning your conversation before you start cuts down on back-and-forth.

Flow

Refer back instead of repeating

Most tools hold context within a conversation. Point back to something you already said rather than re-pasting it.

Clarity

Front-load your context

Give the full picture, background, requirements, audience, in your first message. A slow reveal takes longer than a complete ask.

Clarity

Plan before you build

Outline the approach first. Ask the model to think it through with you before asking it to produce the final thing.

Clarity

Review before you send

A clear ask the first time beats three follow-up clarifications. Worth a quick second look before you hit enter, it's a small habit that saves a round trip.

Not a tradeoff

Efficient and better aren't opposites

The habits that get you a better answer on the first try tend to be the same habits that keep the back-and-forth short. It makes sense once you think about it: being vague doesn't actually save you effort, it just moves that effort to a second message.

Asking for help with a deck
Vague

"Can you help with this deck?"

Specific

"Ten-slide exec summary of Q3 pipeline for VPs of Sales. Direct tone, no more than 200 words a slide."

Debugging something
Vague

"This isn't working, can you fix it?"

Specific

"Here's the error, the relevant file, and what I already tried. Something's blocking the async call."

Editing a draft
Vague

"Make this better."

Specific

"Tighten this to 150 words. Keep both data points. Cut the intro paragraph."

Multiple model providers, including Anthropic and OpenAI, have published research pointing to the same thing: closing a long or complex prompt with a clear, direct ask improves response quality, in some published tests by as much as 30%.

Being specific up front takes about the same effort as being vague. It just pays off sooner instead of later.

The training segment

Good, better, best

Same three tasks, three ways to ask. These read the same whether you're building a report, a deck, or a script, tap a task to open it up.

Summarizing a long thread or document
Good

"Summarize this."

Gets a generic summary. Misses the specific decision you needed, so you follow up twice to get something usable.

Better

"Summarize this thread. I need the decisions made and who owns each follow-up."

Targeted extraction. Usually done in one pass.

Best

"Summarize into 3 bullets: decision, owner, deadline. Flag anything still unresolved." Saved as a reusable template.

Same single read, but the setup cost is paid once instead of every time you do this.

The document only gets read once either way. What changes is how many times you re-explain what you needed. Good path: 1 doc read × 2–3 messages. Best path: 1 doc read × 1 message.
Troubleshooting something that isn't working
Good

Code: "This isn't working, can you fix it?"

Spreadsheet: "This formula's broken, can you fix it?"

Either way, the model asks clarifying questions, you paste more, then it guesses.

Better

Code: "Here's the code, the exact error, and what I already tried."

Spreadsheet: "Here's the formula, what result I expected, and what it's returning instead."

Usually solved in the first response.

Best

Code: "Here's the code, the exact error, what I tried, and what a fix should NOT break."

Spreadsheet: "Here's the formula, the expected vs. actual result, and which other cells reference this one."

Cuts the likely second round where the first fix breaks something else.

Most of the extra back-and-forth here comes from guessing, not from the original ask. Good path often runs 3+ exchanges before landing on a real fix. Best path front-loads the constraints and usually lands in one.
Turning a rough draft into a polished one
Good

"Make this better."

Ambiguous "better" produces a full rewrite you now have to evaluate and re-request.

Better

"Tighten this to 200 words. Keep the two data points. Cut the intro."

Specific and targeted. Usually a single pass.

Best

"Tighten to 200 words, keep the two data points, cut the intro, match the tone of [reference]."

Same pass, no follow-up "actually, can you match our usual tone" round.

A full rewrite means going back through the entire draft. A targeted edit only touches what actually needed to change.

Built in-house, not curated

One framework, four questions

Everything else on this page came from outside Deltek. This one's ours, built by our own AI Adoption & Enablement team. Four questions, asked in order, that turn a vague idea into a prompt that's actually ready to use before you type a word to Claude.

STEP 1 · CONTEXT
What's actually going on?

The background situation. What already exists, what constraints or history matter, what Claude needs to know before it can actually help.

STEP 2 · ROLE
Who should Claude be for this?

A senior engineer, a skeptical editor, a specific kind of expert. "No special role needed" is a perfectly good answer too.

STEP 3 · INTERVIEW
The step most people skip

Before you ask for the deliverable, ask yourself what a smart collaborator would ask you first. Surface the assumptions and edge cases now, not three messages into the conversation.

STEP 4 · TASK
The actual deliverable

What exactly should come out the other end, in what format, and what does "done well" actually look like.

This is really just a more structured version of "front-load your context" and "plan before you build" from earlier on this page. No special tool required, just walk through the four questions yourself before writing anything long or important.

# CRIT Super Prompt Builder

You are helping the user build a detailed, ready-to-send prompt using the CRIT framework: **C**ontext, **R**ole, **I**nterview, **T**ask. The output is a prompt for the user to paste into another Claude conversation (or any LLM) — not an answer to the underlying task itself.

## The four categories

- **Context** — the background situation. What's going on, what already exists, what constraints or history matter, what materials the target Claude will need.
- **Role** — who should the target Claude be for this task. What expertise, perspective, or persona will produce the best output. If no special role is needed, that's a valid answer too.
- **Interview** — the questions a smart collaborator would ask before starting. This surfaces hidden assumptions and edge cases *now*, so the eventual Claude doesn't have to guess or ask mid-task.
- **Task** — the actual deliverable. What exactly should get produced, in what format, and what does "done well" look like.

## Process

**Step 1 — Ask one category at a time, conversationally.**

Go in order: Context, then Role, then Interview, then Task. For each category:
- Ask a short, direct question in your own words (don't just recite the definition above).
- Wait for the user's answer before moving to the next category.
- If an answer is thin or ambiguous in a way that would change the final prompt, ask one quick follow-up before moving on. Don't interrogate — one follow-up max per category.
- For **Interview** specifically: don't just ask "what questions do you have." Prompt the user to think like they're being interviewed — surface the assumptions, edge cases, or unstated constraints someone would need to know. If the user is stuck, offer 2-3 candidate questions based on what they've said in Context/Role and ask them to answer those.

Keep each question tight. No preamble, no re-explaining the framework unless asked.

**Step 2 — Draft the super prompt.**

Once all four categories are answered, assemble them into a single, well-structured prompt using this shape (adapt headers/wording to fit, but keep the four-part structure clear):

## Context
[synthesized from the user's answer — don't just paste it verbatim, tighten it]

## Role
[the persona/expertise framing, stated as an instruction — e.g., "Act as a senior X" — or omitted if the user said no role is needed]

## Task
[the deliverable, stated first and imperatively, with format and success criteria as a short list]

## Things to get right
[the answers from the Interview step, converted into constraints or clarifications the target Claude should already know — not phrased as open questions anymore, since they've been answered]

Don't just concatenate the user's raw answers — write it the way a well-structured prompt should read, task-first, constraints as a list, no narrative padding.

**Step 3 — Run it through prompt-optimizer automatically.**

Immediately after drafting, apply the prompt-optimizer skill's process to your own draft, in the same turn — don't wait for the user to ask. Follow its Steps 1-5 (extract core spec, flag fluff, rewrite, self-check, report). Skip Fiscal Mode unless the user's Context/Task answers included pasted bulk documents, repeated static context, or an obvious model mismatch — if so, run Fiscal Mode too and report those flags.

**Step 4 — Deliver.**

Output, in order:
1. The final optimized super prompt in a code block, ready to copy-paste.
2. A brief "what changed in optimization" note, keep this short, this isn't the main deliverable.

Don't add extra commentary, don't ask if they want revisions unless something in their answers was genuinely ambiguous — if it's ambiguous, flag the specific ambiguity inline rather than opening a generic "let me know if you'd like changes."

## Notes

- If the user already gave you Context/Role/Interview/Task info earlier in the conversation, don't re-ask — confirm what you've inferred in one line per category and move straight to drafting.
- If the user wants to skip a category (e.g., "no role needed"), accept that and move on — don't push back.
- This skill produces a prompt for later use, not an in-conversation answer. If the user's request is actually just "do this task for me," that's not this skill — just do the task.

Specific to Claude

Which model, when

This is the "match the model to the task" quick hit from earlier, expanded. As of today, our Claude Desktop gives us three models to pick from: Haiku 4.5 and two Sonnet versions. Picking the right one isn't about being cheap, it's about matching the tool to the job in front of you. If you're working in Claude Code instead of the chat app, there's a fourth option, covered in the power users section below. (If any of this changes, the version note at the bottom of this page will say so.)

HAIKU 4.5
The quick one

Built for speed. Reach for it when the task is simple and you want the answer fast, not when you need deep analysis.

Good for
  • Quick email or message drafts
  • Reformatting or cleaning up a document
  • Simple lookups and yes/no questions
  • Sorting or triaging a big batch of items
SONNET
The everyday one

The default for a reason. Fast enough to feel interactive, capable enough for real work. Most tasks belong here, whichever version you're on.

Good for
  • Writing and editing reports, emails, decks
  • Everyday coding: reviews, small features, debugging
  • Summarizing a document or meeting notes
  • Most day-to-day research and analysis
You'll see two Sonnet options, here's the difference
Sonnet 4.6

The previous version. Still solid, still available. Fine to keep using if that's what a saved workflow already points to.

Sonnet 5

The newer version of the same tier: more capable on multi-step and reasoning-heavy work, and generally the stronger everyday pick.

Default to Sonnet 5 for anything new. There's little reason to reach for 4.6 on a fresh task.

A rule of thumb worth keeping: start on Sonnet 5 for real work. If the task is genuinely simple and repetitive, drop down to Haiku instead. This is specific to what we currently have access to in Claude, if you're working in another tool day to day, look for its equivalent tiers.
